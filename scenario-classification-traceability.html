Research note · Traceability and scenario classification

Why autonomous vehicle safety
fails without scenario classification

Autonomous systems can detect incidents, simulate them, retrain models, and deploy updates across fleets. What remains less consistent is the layer in between: how incidents are classified, connected, and measured over time. At scale, safety depends not only on fixing problems, but on proving that a class of risk is actually decreasing across real-world operations.

Incidents are not the hardest part. Inconsistency is.

Most autonomous vehicle programs can detect events. Vehicles generate telemetry, operators flag unusual behavior, and engineering teams can often reconstruct what happened with reasonable confidence. The harder problem begins after that initial detection.

The same event may be described in three different ways. Engineering sees a perception issue. Operations sees unexpected vehicle behavior. Safety sees a risk. All three descriptions may be correct. But they do not automatically form a usable system.

Without a consistent way to classify incidents, organisations struggle to answer basic questions over time: how often is this happening, where is it happening, what changed after the last occurrence, and is the underlying risk actually lower today than it was before.

Engineering view

Focuses on component behavior, model performance, and technical cause.

Operational view

Focuses on what the vehicle did, what the operator observed, and how the mission was affected.

Safety view

Focuses on hazard, exposure, consequence, and whether risk remains acceptable.

Why fixing a single event is not the same as reducing risk

A common instinct is to treat incidents individually. A specific event occurs. A simulation is built. A model is retrained. A software update is deployed. If the event does not reappear, the issue is considered resolved.

That may be sufficient for engineering validation. It is not sufficient for system-level proof.

Real-world environments do not repeat themselves exactly. The same intersection presents different lighting conditions, different traffic patterns, different occlusions, and different human behavior across time. What appears as a single event is usually part of a broader pattern.

A shadow misclassification at one intersection is not only a shadow problem at one intersection. It belongs to a wider class of low-contrast perception challenges. A hesitation event at one unprotected turn is not only about that turn. It belongs to a class of uncertain prediction and interaction scenarios.

The practical shift is this: safety is not proven by eliminating a single incident. Safety is proven by reducing the frequency and severity of a class of incidents across real-world conditions.

The missing layer: scenario classification

Scenario classification provides the structure that turns raw events into measurable units of risk. A scenario is not fully defined until it captures four things in a way that different teams can use consistently.

  • Operational description — what the vehicle did in the field
  • Technical cause — why the system behaved that way
  • Context conditions — under what environmental and traffic conditions it tends to occur
  • Safety impact — why the behavior matters from a risk standpoint

When those layers are aligned, similar events can be grouped into a scenario class rather than treated as isolated anecdotes. That enables trend analysis, version-to-version comparison, and more defensible claims about improvement.

From incident to proof

Once scenario classes are defined consistently, they can be connected to the operational lifecycle that follows every event.

This is where traceability becomes meaningful. The point is not simply to show that a fix was deployed. It is to connect incidents, scenario classes, corrective actions, software versions, and post-deployment outcomes into one coherent chain.

Without that chain, organisations can say they improved the system. With that chain, they can show where risk exposure changed, how recurrence shifted, and whether safety claims remain credible across operations.

Why this matters for scale

As deployments expand, the external questions become more structured. Cities, regulators, insurers, and partners do not only ask what happened. They ask what changed afterward, how recurrence is prevented, and whether the organisation can demonstrate similar control across fleets and markets.

Those questions cannot be answered consistently with raw logs or ad hoc incident writeups. They require a stable method for grouping events, tracking corrective actions, and measuring change over time.

That is why scenario classification is not a documentation exercise. It is a prerequisite for scalable safety governance.

A more useful benchmark for maturity

Mature AV organisations do more than resolve incidents. They can show that a scenario class is becoming less frequent, less severe, or better controlled over time. They can point to a timeline of evidence: what happened, how it was classified, what changed, where it was deployed, and what the trend looks like afterward.

That is a much higher bar than fixing a bug. It is also the bar that matters when autonomy moves from demonstration to infrastructure.

Improvement is expected. Proof is what builds trust.

The operational implication

Autonomous systems do not scale on technical capability alone. They scale on the ability to demonstrate that risks are understood, classified consistently, and reduced over time in a way that is traceable across teams and deployments.

That is why scenario classification deserves more attention. Not because it makes incident reports cleaner, but because it provides the structure required to turn incidents into measurable evidence of safety improvement.
