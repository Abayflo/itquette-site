THE ROAD AVs ACTUALLY NEED

The U.S. does not need to rebuild 4.2 million miles of road for autonomous vehicles to scale. It needs to make those roads legible, measurable, and governable — and the gap between where infrastructure stands today and where rapid deployment demands it to be is wider than the industry publicly admits.

Every serious AV infrastructure conversation starts with the same question: how much does the road need to change before the vehicle can safely operate on it? The honest answer is more interesting than either optimists or critics tend to admit.

AVs can start on today's roads. Waymo operates in Phoenix, San Francisco, and Austin without waiting for those cities to install roadside sensors at every intersection. But operating in a constrained ODD and scaling commercially across an urban population are different propositions. The infrastructure gap that barely matters for a pilot becomes a ceiling for a business.

Poor infrastructure does not make autonomous vehicles impossible. It makes their scalability harder to defend — to investors, to regulators, and to the public watching every edge case play out in real time.

01
The Core Distinction

Legible roads before smart roads

The industry default — that V2X connectivity and sensor-laden intersections must precede large-scale deployment — misreads the actual constraint. NHTSA's ADS guidance does not require intelligent infrastructure. It explicitly encourages governments to maintain signs, signals, and lane markings. The operative word is maintain, not transform.

The first wave of necessary infrastructure investment is not futuristic. It is deferred maintenance. Worn lane striping, obscured signage, poorly timed signals, and degraded pavement are the actual perception challenges that narrow operating domains and drive interventions — not the absence of roadside radars.

02
Highest Leverage Upgrade

Intersections matter more than highways

If there is a single physical infrastructure element that disproportionately determines AV scaling velocity, it is the intersection. Intersections are where pedestrians cross, cyclists interact, emergency vehicles assert priority, transit turns, and signals behave inconsistently — and where public incidents are most visible.

FHWA simulation data shows intelligent intersections can reduce rear-end conflicts by 46–54% with only 10% connected vehicle market penetration. Sharing SPaT signal phase data can cut fuel consumption by up to 40% through speed smoothing alone. The case for prioritising intersection modernisation above almost any other single investment is analytically clear.

03
The Federal Bet

V2X deployment — where it stands

USDOT released a national V2X deployment plan in 2024, framing vehicle-to-everything communication as a foundational layer for both AV scaling and connected mobility. The data makes clear: the U.S. is in early innings, with coverage concentrated in pilot corridors rather than operational networks.

The $60M in FHWA V2X grants to Arizona, Texas, and Utah are the clearest signal of federal intent. Arizona's project alone covers 750 physical and virtual roadside units, 400 onboard units, and intersections across Phoenix, Tolleson, and Avondale — co-located with the most active robotaxi market in the country. The gap between operational and planned RSUs signals that procurement intention significantly outpaces completed installation.

04
The Invisible Gap

The most critical upgrade is digital, not concrete

The most underappreciated infrastructure challenge for rapid AV deployment is neither roads nor signals. It is machine-readable public data. AV operators require structured, real-time feeds covering work zones, lane closures, signal status, special events, curb restrictions, and temporary traffic control changes.

In most U.S. cities, this data exists in some form — but it is fragmented across departments, inconsistently formatted, and unavailable via API. An AV fleet that never received a work zone closure navigates around it hundreds of times, generating remote assistance events, interventions, and blocked traffic — until someone updates the map manually. That is the operational reality, and it has nothing to do with the quality of the road surface.

The AV-ready city of 2030 will not be defined by its sensors. It will be defined by its operational data discipline — the consistency, timeliness, and machine-readability of the information it publishes about itself.

06
Risk Landscape

Where the gaps become ceilings

The consequence of infrastructure shortfalls is not catastrophic failure — it is chronic friction. Smaller ODDs. More conservative vehicle behaviour. More remote assistance events. More conflict with cyclists, pedestrians, and buses competing for degraded curb space. This friction is measurable before it becomes visible to the public — and it is where the scalability case for each deployment either holds or quietly erodes.

Critical
Level 4 performance in work zone environments
Temporary lane shifts, flaggers, cones, and changing rules cannot be resolved by static maps. Without real-time work zone data feeds, AVs encounter these as novel situations on every pass — generating interventions at scale.
Critical
Curbside congestion in high-density zones
Bus stops, bike lanes, loading zones, and passenger drop-off all compete for the same curb space. Robotaxis without designated zones create the conditions for the most visible and politically damaging AV incidents.
Watch closely
Regulatory patience in post-incident cities
San Francisco's regulatory environment after the 2023 Cruise suspension is materially more cautious. A serious infrastructure-related incident risks suspension of entire programmes — not just the responsible operator.
Watch closely
Data fragmentation across city departments
Work zone data, special event closures, and temporary signage are managed across incompatible systems. Until cities treat machine-readable operational data as core infrastructure, this fragmentation creates daily friction for every fleet operator.
More manageable
Highway and arterial physical condition
Major highway infrastructure in AV operating cities is generally adequate. Current robotaxi ODDs are chosen, in part, to avoid the worst road conditions. The physical maintenance gap is real but manageable at corridor scale.
More manageable
Nationwide V2X coverage gaps
AVs are not currently designed to depend on V2X to operate safely — it is an enhancement layer, not a dependency. The gap matters more for scaling efficiency than near-term operational safety.

AV readiness is not only about upgrading roads. It is about knowing which road conditions repeatedly create risk — and proving that operators and cities are reducing those risks over time.

Five things the U.S. actually needs to build

Rapid AV deployment does not require rebuilding America's road network. It requires five targeted modernisation investments — and the discipline to treat infrastructure as an ongoing operational system, not a one-time capital project.

01
Physical legibility
Clear, consistent, maintained lane markings, signage, signal heads, and work zone treatments in AV operating domains. The foundational layer everything else depends on.
02
Connected intersections & V2X corridors
SPaT, MAP, and phase data at signalised intersections in dense urban and freight zones. The highest-leverage digital upgrade for urban AV performance.
03
Machine-readable operational data
Work zone feeds, curb access rules, signal data, event and closure information — structured, timely, and accessible via API. Where most cities are furthest behind.
04
Curb and drop-off infrastructure
Robotaxis need safe, legally defined places to stop. The curb is already contested. Designated zones are a prerequisite for commercial robotaxi density.
05 — The shift
Operational governance and traceability
AVs can drive on today's roads. They will scale on roads that can communicate, be measured, and be governed. The infrastructure investment case for rapid AV deployment is not primarily a construction case — it is a data and operational discipline case. The cities that understand this earliest will attract the deployments that matter most.
