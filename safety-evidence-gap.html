The AV Safety Gap Is Not About Accidents.
It Is About Evidence.

Why safety improvement and safety acceptance are not always the same thing.
May 2026 · ITquette Autonomous Lab · Framework · Safety governance

Operators, regulators, insurers, and the public all care deeply about autonomous vehicle safety. But they often evaluate it using entirely different forms of evidence. That gap — not technology, not performance — may be the harder problem to solve.

For years, autonomous vehicle safety discussions have centred on a familiar question: Are autonomous vehicles safer than human drivers?

The industry has invested enormous effort trying to answer it. Operators publish safety reports. Researchers analyse crash data. Regulators evaluate deployment programmes. Insurers study emerging risk patterns.

Yet despite growing evidence that autonomous systems may reduce certain types of crashes and injuries, public trust remains stubbornly low.

What if the challenge facing autonomous vehicles is no longer only about safety performance? What if it is increasingly about how safety is evaluated, interpreted, and trusted?

The more we examined discussions surrounding autonomous mobility, the more a pattern emerged. Operators, regulators, insurers, and the public all care deeply about safety — but they rely on very different forms of evidence when deciding whether a system is safe.

This is the basis of the Safety Evidence Gap Framework™.

The Safety Evidence Gap Framework™

The assumption behind most AV discussions

Many discussions about autonomous vehicles follow a simple logic:

The prevailing model

Improved safety performance → Increased trust → Wider deployment

If autonomous vehicles become demonstrably safer than human drivers, adoption should naturally follow. The assumption seems reasonable. Reality is more complicated.

While operators continue publishing encouraging safety statistics, public scepticism remains significant. The challenge may not be that safety is not improving. The challenge may be that different stakeholders evaluate safety using different evidence — and those evidence models rarely align.

Four stakeholders. Four evidence models.

Each group is asking a genuinely different question — and reaching for genuinely different data to answer it.

01

"Are we reducing risk?"
Crash and injury reduction rates Intervention frequency Fleet exposure data Operational performance metrics
The system is becoming safer.

"Can future losses be predicted?"
Claims frequency and severity Exposure and trend data Loss predictability Risk pricing stability
Risk can be measured and priced.
04
Public
"Why should I trust this system?"
Personal experience Visible incidents and media coverage Perceived control Emotional confidence
The system feels trustworthy — or it does not.

The public rarely evaluates safety through fleet-wide statistics. Highly visible incidents shape perception disproportionately — regardless of what the data shows at scale. Insurers are not primarily concerned with miles driven or safety cases. Their concern is financial risk, and until patterns are stable enough to price, scepticism persists.

The same word. Different evidence.

Scenario

"Autonomous vehicles reduced injury crashes by 80% compared with human drivers."

Operators view this as compelling evidence of progress. Insurers ask to see claims data. Regulators ask to review the supporting safety case. The public focuses on a recent incident that received national media attention.

Everyone is discussing safety. They are not discussing the same evidence.

This is not a communication failure. It is a structural one. Each stakeholder group has legitimate reasons to trust the evidence it trusts. The gap is not closed by better marketing. It is closed — slowly — by building evidence that is legible across all four models simultaneously.

The challenge is not that autonomous vehicles are unsafe. The challenge is that the evidence used to demonstrate safety is not universally trusted.

Three gaps. One underlying problem.

As autonomous systems mature, this creates a distinction that matters for how the industry thinks about progress:

Gap one
Safety performance
Gap two
Safety confidence
Gap three
Safety acceptance

A system can improve on all three dimensions independently. An operator can reduce crashes while regulators remain uncertain about governance. Regulators can approve a deployment while the public remains unconvinced. Insurers can price a risk while public trust lags far behind.

Technical performance alone will not close these gaps. The ability to communicate safety improvement across all four stakeholder groups — using evidence each group finds credible — may become equally important to deployment success.

Questions worth holding

  1. If autonomous vehicles become demonstrably safer than human drivers, what evidence will be required for each stakeholder group to believe it?

  2. Who is responsible for translating safety evidence across operators, regulators, insurers, and the public — and does that role currently exist in most organisations?

  3. As autonomous systems scale, will technical capability remain the primary challenge — or will evidence alignment become the harder problem?

The answers may play a significant role in determining how autonomous mobility evolves — not just technically, but socially and politically — in the years ahead.
